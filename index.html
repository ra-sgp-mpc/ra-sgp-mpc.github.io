RA-SGP-MPC: Risk-aware speed adaptation for path tracking of agricultural robots under localisation and model uncertainty

Manuscript under review

Video Method Results

Abstract

Path-tracking accuracy determines the quality of agricultural field operations, and travel speed the field capacity. Since a higher speed means a larger error, robots usually drive at a fixed speed chosen for the most difficult sections of a route, such as headland turns and stretches of degraded localisation, and are slower than necessary elsewhere. In most agricultural controllers, model uncertainty at most makes the steering more cautious, and localisation uncertainty stays in the estimator. This paper presents risk-aware sparse-GP model predictive control (RA-SGP-MPC), which uses both uncertainties to set the travel speed. A sparse Gaussian process (GP) vehicle model and an extended Kalman filter predict the lateral error and its uncertainty, and the robot drives at the fastest speed whose predicted error stays within an 8 cm error budget with 95% probability. The steering is solved as a convex quadratic programme, and the controller runs in real time on an embedded computer. At two field sites, on serpentine and spiral routes with and without injected localisation noise, RA-SGP-MPC was compared with four fixed-speed controllers, including a GP-based MPC. With RTK localisation, it completed the routes in 11.1–14.8% less time than all baselines, with the lowest root-mean-square lateral error (RMSE). Under localisation noise, it slowed down in the noise sections, reduced the RMSE by 20–33% relative to the best baseline and still finished first. Its 95th-percentile error stayed within the budget in every run. Both uncertainties can decide where a field robot drives fast, raising field capacity without losing accuracy.

Fixed speed and risk-aware speed

The lateral error at a given speed is not the same along a route. It grows in headland turns, where slip and the lag of the drive make the vehicle model least accurate, and where obstructions degrade the satellite localisation. A fixed speed must therefore be chosen for the most difficult section and is slower than necessary everywhere else. RA-SGP-MPC predicts the lateral error and its uncertainty on the path ahead and slows down only where the predicted error approaches the tolerance.

Method

Four components run once per control period of 0.1 s. An extended Kalman filter (EKF) fuses the reported pose with its covariance, which is calibrated against a stationary recording. A sparse GP learns the residual of a feedback-linearised nominal model that includes the identified actuator response. A risk-aware speed layer predicts the lateral error and its uncertainty for a set of candidate speeds, and a convex steering MPC tracks the path at the selected speed.

Speed selection

For each candidate speed between 1.0 and 1.5 m/s, the error is predicted over the next 4 s. The robot keeps the fastest candidate whose 95% band stays within the budget, and the band grows with both the model and the localisation uncertainty.

Field experiments

The test platform is a skid-steer field robot with a dual-antenna RTK-GNSS/INS receiver that reports the pose with its covariance. All controllers ran at 10 Hz on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin with the same command limits. The experiments were carried out in a harvested wheat field (site 1) and a tilled field (site 2). At each site, the robot drove a serpentine route of six 45 m passes joined by headland turns and an inward spiral, with RTK localisation and with localisation noise injected in marked sections, together with its covariance.

Experiment 1

Time and accuracy with RTK localisation

RA-SGP-MPC drove fast where its predicted error was small and slowed down where it was large. On the serpentine, it drove the passes at about 1.5 m/s and slowed to about 1.0 m/s in the headland turns. On the spiral, its speed decreased gradually as the curvature increased towards the inner laps. It completed the serpentine 14.1–14.8% and the spiral 11.1–12.9% sooner than the baselines. In the headland turns, its RMSE was 2.78 and 4.38 cm at the two sites, against 3.65–4.86 and 5.06–8.19 cm for the baselines, and on the passes, which it drove about 0.3 m/s faster, its RMSE was still the lowest.

Experiment 2

Response to degraded localisation

Noise was added to the pose in marked sections and reported together with its covariance, as when the RTK solution degrades near tree lines and buildings. RA-SGP-MPC slowed to about 1.0 m/s in each noise section, even on straight passes where the route geometry alone did not require it, and drove the remaining segments at about 1.4–1.5 m/s. Inside the noise sections of the serpentine, the RMSE of the MPC baselines rose to 4.04–5.75 cm, whereas that of RA-SGP-MPC was 3.24 and 2.91 cm. Over the whole route, its RMSE was 31.8 and 33.4% below that of the best baseline on the serpentine and 19.5 and 21.0% below on the spiral, and it still finished first.

Error budget and computation time

In every run of RA-SGP-MPC, with and without localisation noise, the 95th percentile of its error was below the 8 cm budget, at most 5.9 cm over a route and 6.2 cm inside the noise sections, and its error exceeded the budget on at most 1.1% of a route. Its median computation time per control step was 24–41 ms, most of it for the error predictions, and its 99th percentile was at most 48.8 ms, within the control period of 0.1 s.

Implications for field operations

At equal working width and field efficiency, the shorter completion times correspond to a travel-time-based field capacity that is 12.5–17.4% higher with RTK localisation. Since the time is gained on the passes, the gain increases with the row length, to about 22% for 300 m rows. The user specifies the tolerance of the operation instead of a travel speed, and a tighter tolerance makes the speed selection more restrictive without retuning the steering.

Conclusions

The uncertainty of a learned vehicle model and of the localisation can be used to decide where an agricultural robot drives fast, rather than only how cautiously it steers. RA-SGP-MPC combines both uncertainties in a prediction of the lateral error and drives at the fastest speed whose predicted error stays within an error budget with 95% probability. Because the speed is chosen before the steering is optimised, the steering remains a convex QP, and the controller runs in real time on an embedded computer. In field experiments at two sites, the robot slowed down only where the predicted error approached the budget, in the headland turns and in the sections of degraded localisation, and gained time on the rest of the route without losing accuracy.
